# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This manuscript develops and cross-validates a constrained lifecycle optimization framework for low-carbon structural concrete, grounded in an approximately 11-year natural field weathering dataset (45 mixtures, 353 phenolphthalein carbonation depth measurements across sheltered and unsheltered microclimates) in tropical Chennai, India. By integrating an air-normalized multivariable Bolomey compressive strength model, log-linear natural carbonation kinetic regressions with explicit $+1\sigma$ out-of-sample predictive uncertainty buffers, stoichiometric $\text{CO}_2$ uptake accounting (EN 16757 Module B1), and prescriptive national code boundaries (EN 206 and IS 456), the study evaluates discrete and continuous mixtures across 18 structural design scenarios to minimize net 50-year lifecycle embodied carbon.

## Strengths
* **Integration of Decade-Long Natural Tropical Weathering with Lifecycle Optimization:** The study bridges a major gap in sustainable structural engineering by calibrating continuous mix optimization directly against an 11-year natural tropical field exposure dataset across sheltered and monsoon-exposed outdoor microclimates, avoiding the microstructural distortions of short-term accelerated carbonation tests.
* **Diagnostic Air-Void Normalization and Out-of-Sample Predictive Buffers:** By normalizing the Bolomey strength relation for measured fresh air content ($0.9\%\text{--}6.6\%$), the framework resolves the apparent breakdown of classical strength-porosity trends in unadmixed test batches ($R^2$ improving from $0.071$ to $0.734$) and enforces explicit Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation ($+1\sigma$) screening margins to prevent continuous optimizers from exploiting regression scatter at design space boundaries.
* **Rigorous Kuhn-Tucker Slackness Proof and Actionable Decarbonization Decomposition:** The manuscript uses Kuhn-Tucker optimality conditions to prove that carbonation durability—rather than 28-day compressive strength—is the active binding constraint for ordinary low-carbon structural grades, while path-dependently decomposing carbon savings into binder/water reduction versus binary fly-ash–slag blending and confirming that use-phase $\text{CO}_2$ uptake offsets only $3.8\%\text{--}7.7\%$ of initial emissions.

## Weaknesses
* **Unmodeled Total Binder Sensitivity in Strength and Kinetic Regressions:** Because both the Bolomey strength regression and the log-linear carbonation kinetic equations omit total binder content ($B$) as a predictor, the models assume invariant strength and carbonation resistance as $B$ is reduced from $310$ to $280\text{ kg/m}^3$ (or $220\text{ kg/m}^3$); please consider further discussing how the loss of absolute volumetric alkaline reserve ($\text{CaO}_{\text{reactive}}\text{ per m}^3$) affects multi-decadal kinetics when attributing $70.8\%\text{--}87.1\%$ of continuous carbon savings to binder reduction.
* **Collinearity and Unobserved Corner Extrapolation at Low Water-Binder Ratios:** The continuous optima rely on achieving $w/b = 0.45$ with $2.0\%$ entrapped air via polycarboxylate ether (PCE) superplasticizers, yet all eight laboratory training batches at $w/b = 0.45$ were unadmixed and averaged $5.1\%$ air ($h_{00} \approx 0.22$); the authors might consider expanding on the estimation sensitivity of the Bolomey slope under this collinearity, particularly since enforcing a textbook air penalty ($\approx -2.2\text{ MPa/\%}$ instead of $-4.354\text{ MPa/\%}$) causes the strength constraint to bind and reduces fly ash feasibility (Reference Paper: Properties of Concrete, Adam M. Neville, 2011).
* **Sensitivity of the 50% SCM Solution Space to Compounded Code Safety Margins:** Pinning the 50-year phenolphthalein neutralization front directly to nominal cover ($25\text{--}35\text{ mm}$) leaves zero margin for execution tolerances ($\Delta c_{\text{dev}} = 5\text{--}10\text{ mm}$) or the pH offset between phenolphthalein clearing ($\text{pH} \approx 9.0$) and steel depassivation ($\text{pH} \approx 10.5\text{--}11.5$); simultaneously enforcing these physical offsets alongside full target-mean strength margins ($1.65s \approx 6.6\text{--}8.25\text{ MPa}$) collapses the feasible $50\%$ SCM space at $25\text{--}30\text{ mm}$ nominal covers unless higher total covers are specified (Reference Paper: fib Model Code for Concrete Structures 2020, International Federation for Structural Concrete (fib), 2023; Reference Paper: IS 10262:2019 – Concrete Mix Proportioning Guidelines, Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), 2019).
* **Missing Quantitative Benchmarking Against Site-Specific 5-Year Tropical Models and Exposure Resistance Classes:** While the introduction mentions contextualizing kinetics against prior site models, the manuscript would be strengthened by quantitatively comparing its 11-year $d_{50}$ projections and cover selections against the IIT Madras experimental team's dedicated 5-year tropical carbonation design model from the exact same Chennai rooftop site, as well as mapping the buffered kinetic rates onto performance-based Carbonation Exposure Resistance Classes (XRC) in second-generation Eurocode 2 (Reference Paper: Carbonation model for concretes with fly ash, slag, and limestone calcined clay - using accelerated and five-year natural exposure data, Sundar Rathnarajan, B.S. Dhanya, Radhakrishna G. Pillai, Ravindra Gettu, Manu Santhanam, 2022; Reference Paper: Impact of different climates on the resistance of concrete to natural carbonation, Quoc Huy Vu et al., 2019; Reference Paper: EN 1992-1-1:2023 Eurocode 2: Design of concrete structures, European Committee for Standardization (CEN), 2023).
* **Methodological Asymmetry in Uncertainty Buffers and Sensitivity Parameterizations:** The $+15\%$ kinetic uncertainty margin is derived from unadjusted sheltered in-sample RMSE ($\exp(0.1411)$) rather than exposure-specific $\text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}}$ values ($+18.1\%$ for XC3 and $+19.3\%$ for XC4) as done for strength, and please consider clarifying the algorithmic rationale for using the quadratic decoupled $\text{CO}_2$ uptake approximation ($\overline{\text{CaO}}\cdot\bar{\alpha}_c$) instead of the exact linear sum alongside the emission factor and constraint parameterizations in the Table V sensitivity scenarios.

* **Incomplete Summary of Carbon Reduction Ranges Across All 18 Design Scenarios (Section I-B, Contribution 4, Page 2):**
  In Section I-B, Contribution (4) states that the study executes constrained continuous and discrete lifecycle optimization across 18 design scenarios, *"demonstrating 9.6%–30.2% carbon reductions over discrete baselines depending on cover depth (9.6% at 30 mm, 19.8%–30.2% at 25 mm)."* A potential concern regarding internal consistency is that this 9.6%–30.2% range reflects only specific sheltered exposure scenarios (Section V-C and Table IV-A) and omits the unsheltered outdoor exposure scenarios (Table IV-B) that make up half of the 18 evaluated design scenarios:

* **Scope Delimitation Regarding Limestone Calcined Clay (LC3) Systems (Section I-A & I-B, Pages 1–2):**

However, calcined clay is absent from the empirical dataset, strength and carbonation regressions, and lifecycle optimization framework, which evaluate only FA, GGBS, limestone filler (LS), and PO. While mentioning limestone calcined clay in the general motivation is factually accurate, listing it alongside the specific SCMs modeled in the paper without an explicit scope boundary is a potential concern for clarity—especially since the same IIT Madras research group concurrently conducted a parallel 10-year natural carbonation campaign at the Chennai site covering fly ash and LC3 blends. Explicitly noting when transitioning to Section I-B (or at the start of Section II-A) that the empirical optimization scope is bounded to the four SCM classes (FA, GGBS, LS, and PO) present in the 45-mixture cohort of Hule et al. [16], [17]—and briefly remarking on extending the framework to companion long-term tropical $\text{LC}^3$ datasets in Section VII—would clarify this boundary.

#### Minor Corrections and Typos

* **Section I-A (Page 1, Column 2, Paragraph 1):** In the sentence *"Indian cement standards (IS 10262 [4], IS 456 [5]) and European standards (EN 206 [6], EN 197-1 [7]) classify binders by strength grade and constituent limits"*, note that IS 10262 (mix proportioning guidelines), IS 456 (structural concrete code of practice), and EN 206 (concrete specification and performance) govern concrete mix design and durability limits rather than cement binder classification itself (which in India is governed by standards such as IS 269 for OPC, IS 1489 for PPC [23], and IS 455 for PSC, analogous to EN 197-1 [7]). * **Potential Concern Regarding Exposure-Specific Kinetic Error and Methodological Asymmetry Between Strength and Kinetic Uncertainty Buffers (Sections III-C, III-D, and IV-B, Pages 4–6):**
  In Section III-C (Page 4, Eq. 3), the manuscript carefully distinguishes between in-sample fit ($\text{RMSE} = 3.78\text{ MPa}$), degrees-of-freedom adjusted residual standard error ($s_e = 4.11\text{ MPa}$), and Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation error ($\text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}} = 4.38\text{ MPa}$), strictly enforcing $+1 \times \text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}}$ ($\Delta f_{\text{margin}} = +4.4\text{ MPa}$) to guard against optimizer exploitation of regression scatter and consumed degrees of freedom. By contrast, in Section III-D (Page 5, Eq. 6) and Section IV-B (Page 6, Eq. 13), the $+1\sigma$ kinetic uncertainty margin $\gamma_{\text{dur}} = 1.15$ is derived solely from the unadjusted *in-sample* RMSE of the sheltered XC3 regression ($\exp(0.1411) = 1.1515 \approx 1.15$) and applied uniformly across both sheltered (XC3) and unsheltered (XC4) exposures. However, Section III-D reports that the out-of-sample LOO-CV error for sheltered XC3 is $\text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}} = 0.1663$ ($\exp(0.1663) = 1.181$, or $+18.1\%$), and that unsheltered XC4 exhibits higher residual scatter across all metrics: in-sample $\text{RMSE} = 0.1552$ ($\exp(0.1552) = 1.168$), $s_e = 0.1667$ ($\exp(0.1667) = 1.181$), and $\text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}} = 0.1765$ ($\exp(0.1765) = 1.193$, or $+19.3\%$).
  * Applying the sheltered in-sample error uniformly slightly under-represents the $+1\sigma$ out-of-sample kinetic uncertainty, particularly for unsheltered XC4 where durability binds under uncertainty at $25\text{ mm}$ cover (Table IV, Part B, Rows 1 and 4, tightening $d_{50,\text{allow}}$ from $25/1.15 = 21.74\text{ mm}$ to $25/1.193 = 20.96\text{ mm}$). Explicitly justifying why the unadjusted sheltered in-sample RMSE ($+15\%$) was selected for $\gamma_{\text{dur}}$ rather than exposure-specific $\exp(\text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}})$ values ($+18.1\%$ for XC3 and $+19.3\%$ for XC4) would reconcile the statistical convention between Equations 3 and 6.

* **Potential Concern Regarding Algorithmic Rationale, Notation, and Solver Specification for the Decoupled Stoichiometric Uptake Approximation (Sections III-E and IV-A, Page 5):**
  In Section III-E (Page 5), Eq. 8 defines stoichiometric $\text{CO}_2$ uptake via the coupled constituent sum $\sum_i f_i \text{CaO}_i \alpha_{c,i}$, where the products $c_i = \text{CaO}_i \alpha_{c,i}$ are fixed constants from Table III. Consequently, for a given binder content $B$ and penetration depth $x_c(50\text{y})$, the exact coupled sum $\sum_i f_i c_i$ is a strictly linear function of the constituent mass fractions $f_i$. The text states that the continuous optimizer instead evaluates the decoupled product $\overline{\text{CaO}}\cdot\bar{\alpha}_c = (\sum_i f_i \text{CaO}_i)(\sum_i f_i \alpha_{c,i})$, which replaces a simple linear dot product with a quadratic bilinear form in $f_i$ and underestimates uptake by $\approx 0.65\text{ kg CO}_2/\text{m}^3$.
  * While the authors rigorously establish via Chebyshev's sum inequality that this decoupled form acts as a valid conservative lower bound, it remains unclear from an algorithmic design standpoint why the optimization script evaluates the quadratic decoupled approximation rather than the exact linear dot product directly.
  * Additionally, Eq. 8 denotes the binder constituent mass fractions as $f_i$, whereas Section IV-A (Eqs. 9–16) denotes the same decision variables as $x_j$ (and $x_i$ in Section IV-A, Paragraph 2), and Section IV does not specify the numerical nonlinear programming algorithm (e.g., Sequential Least Squares Programming [SLSQP] or interior-point), convergence tolerances, or initialization strategy used in `run_constrained_optimization.py` to solve Eq. 9 across all 18 scenarios (especially for the ternary XC4 interior solutions in Table IV-B). Briefly clarifying the rationale for the decoupled objective, confirming that $f_i \equiv x_i$, and stating the numerical solver specification would improve algorithmic clarity and reproducibility.

* **Potential Concern Regarding First-Stage Estimation Variance and Equal Second-Stage Weighting Under Staggered Longitudinal Sampling (Section III-D, Page 5):**
  In Section III-D (Page 5), first-stage carbonation rate coefficients $k$ for each mixture are estimated via OLS through the origin across up to six measurement epochs ($k = \sum(x_{c,i}\sqrt{t_i})/\sum t_i$), and the text notes that *"All $N = 45$ mixtures were weighted equally in the second-stage OLS regressions"* (Eqs. 4 and 5). Because the longitudinal testing campaign was staggered (as documented on Page 2, filling 353 of 540 possible specimen-epoch slots, with Epoch 4 testing all 45 mixes, Epoch 5 testing 25 sheltered and 40 unsheltered, and Epoch 6 testing 38 sheltered and 42 unsheltered at $10.1\text{–}11.5\text{ yr}$), different mixtures were observed across different subsets of epochs and maximum exposure horizons.
  * Under OLS through the origin, the estimation variance of the first-stage slope $\hat{k}_m$ scales inversely with cumulative exposure time ($\text{Var}(\hat{k}_m) = \sigma_{\varepsilon}^2 / \sum_{i \in S_m} t_{i,m}$, which is heavily weighted toward the late epochs at $t = 8.1$ and $11.5\text{ yr}$) and transforms to log space via the delta method as $\text{Var}(\ln \hat{k}_m) \approx \text{Var}(\hat{k}_m)/\hat{k}_m^2$. Thus, the 7 sheltered and 3 unsheltered mixtures missing Epoch 6 readings carry higher first-stage estimation variance and reflect a shorter effective exposure window than mixtures anchored through 11.5 years. While unweighted two-stage regression remains unbiased and is common in the concrete durability literature, briefly noting whether mixtures missing late-epoch readings exhibit comparable residual variance/leverage in Eqs. 4–5—or confirming that weighting the second-stage regression by $\sum t_i$ does not materially alter the SCM kinetic slopes ($\gamma_j$)—would bolster the statistical justification of Section III-D.

* **Potential Concern Regarding Missing Empirical Comparison Against the Site Experimental Team's 5-Year Model and Performance-Based Exposure Resistance Classes (Sections I-B, III-D, IV-B, IV-C, VI, and VII):**
  Two domain comparisons would strengthen the design justification and closing synthesis across the manuscript:
  1. *Comparison with the Site Team's 5-Year Tropical Model:* Section I-B states that the investigation builds upon the 11-year Chennai dataset [16], [17], *"contextualizing empirical kinetics relative to prior tropical models developed by the site experimental team [15]."* While Reference [15] (Vu et al., 2019) analyzes 5-year natural carbonation data across five international exposure sites (including the 45 French/Greek mixtures in Chennai), the site experimental team at IIT Madras (Rathnarajan, Pillai, Gettu, and Santhanam—the core authors of [16] and [17]) also developed a dedicated 5-year service-life prediction and 50-year cover-selection model calibrated at the exact same Chennai rooftop exposure site (Rathnarajan et al., *Cement and Concrete Composites*, vol. 126, p. 104329, 2022, the companion modeling paper cited in the Hule et al. [16] data descriptor). However, Sections III-D, V, VI, and VII evaluate the newly calibrated 11-year multivariable log-linear kinetic models (Eqs. 4 and 5) internally without quantitatively comparing their predicted rate coefficients ($k_{\text{sh}}, k_{\text{un}}$), 50-year carbonation depths ($d_{50}$), SCM contrast factors, or optimal cover/binder selections against the experimental team's prior 5-year tropical models in [15] or Rathnarajan et al. (2022). Incorporating a brief quantitative comparison showing how extending the observation window from 5 to 11 years (reducing the 50-year extrapolation factor from $10\times$ to $\approx 4.3\text{–}4.7\times$) relaxes or tightens 50-year cover recommendations relative to the site team's 5-year framework—or refining the wording in Section I-B—would directly fulfill this stated objective.
  2. *Performance-Based Exposure Resistance Classes (EN 1992-1-1:2023):* In Sections IV-B, IV-C, V-A, VI-A.3, and VI-D, the feasible domain combines legacy prescriptive binder minimums from EN 206:2013 [6] ($B \ge 280\text{ kg/m}^3$ for XC3; $B \ge 300\text{ kg/m}^3$ for XC4) with a performance limit on carbonation depth ($x_c(50\text{y}, \mathbf{x}) \le d_{\text{cover}}/\gamma_{\text{dur}}$, Eq. 13), quantifying a $+23.3\text{–}24.1\text{ kg CO}_2\text{e/m}^3$ ($+21.5\%$) "Structural Binder Penalty" over the unconstrained $B = 220\text{ kg/m}^3$ envelope. Because the second-generation Eurocode 2 (EN 1992-1-1:2023) and *fib* Model Code 2020 [13] specifically address the trade-off between low-clinker concretes and carbonation durability by introducing performance-based Carbonation Exposure Resistance Classes (XRC, defined by the 90th-percentile 50-year carbonation depth/rate linked to $c_{\text{min,dur}}$) to decouple low-clinker qualification from prescriptive minimum binder rules, briefly mapping how the $+1\sigma$ buffered rates ($1.15\,k_{\text{model}}$, representing the $\approx 84\text{th}$ one-sided normal percentile) and continuous optima relate to XRC classifications in Sections IV-C, VI-D, and VII would enhance the practical specification context.
